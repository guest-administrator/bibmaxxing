# JP 3-12 — Joint Cyberspace Operations

**Bib scope:** Chapters I, II, III, IV. This is the capstone joint doctrine for military cyberspace operations — every CWT Chief is expected to carry its framework, terminology, and command relationships in their head.

## Why this reference matters on the exam {#why-it-matters}

JP 3-12 is the doctrinal floor for every other cyber publication on your Bib. NTTP 3-13.1 (Information Operations planning), NWP 3-12 (Navy cyberspace ops), CJCSI 6510.01F (IA/CND), and CJCSM 6510.01B (Cyber Incident Handling) all extend or implement JP 3-12's vocabulary. If you get the JP 3-12 definitions wrong, you will get a cascade of related questions wrong.

The advancement exam will test:

- The **three layers of cyberspace** and the **three primary mission areas** (this is a guaranteed question).
- The difference between **DCO-IDM** and **DCO-RA**, because they are the two most commonly-confused terms on the exam.
- The **command relationship between USCYBERCOM and the service cyber components** — supported vs. supporting language matters.
- How cyberspace ops **integrate into the Joint Planning Process (JOPP)** — not a standalone process.
- How **cyberspace actions** (ISR, Operational Preparation of the Environment, Cyber Attack) differ from the three mission areas.

The current widely-circulated public edition is **JP 3-12, 8 June 2018**. Some COOL references cite JP 3-12(R) — that was the earlier restricted edition.

## Chapter I — The Cyberspace Domain {#chapter-i-cyberspace-domain}

### Definition of cyberspace {#cyberspace-definition}

JP 3-12 defines **cyberspace** as:

> "A global domain within the information environment consisting of the interdependent network of information technology infrastructures and resident data, including the Internet, telecommunications networks, computer systems, and embedded processors and controllers."

Two things to internalize from this definition:

1. Cyberspace is a **domain**, co-equal with land, sea, air, and space. Joint doctrine treats it as a warfighting domain — not a support function.
2. It exists **within the information environment**. The information environment is the superset; cyberspace is one component of it. That is why JP 3-13 (Information Operations) overlaps with JP 3-12 but is scoped differently.

### The three layers of cyberspace {#three-layers}

This is the single most-tested fact from Chapter I. Memorize the layers and what lives in each:

**1. Physical network layer.** The geographic location of network components and the physical infrastructure that carries data — fiber-optic cables, copper, servers, routers, switches, radios, undersea cables, satellites, the buildings they sit in, and the power that feeds them. This is the layer that can be affected by a pipe-bender, a backhoe, or a cruise missile. Sovereignty, property rights, and physical access controls live here.

**2. Logical network layer.** The relationships between nodes that are independent of the specific physical infrastructure. IP addressing, DNS, routing tables, MAC bindings, TCP sessions, application-layer protocols (HTTP, SMTP, TLS) — anything defined by software and configuration rather than geography. This is where most tactical cyber operations take place, because the logical layer is fungible: a packet can reach a given logical endpoint via many different physical paths.

**3. Cyber-persona layer.** Digital representations of actual people or entities — user accounts, email addresses, social-media handles, PKI certificates, cryptographic key material, and the online identities tied to real humans or organizations. One person can own many personas; one persona can be controlled by many people; personas can be created, moved, and discarded. Attribution problems start here.

A common exam-trap: **"operational technology layer"**, **"spectrum layer"**, or **"access layer"** are **NOT** layers of cyberspace. If one of those shows up as an answer choice, it is the distractor.

### Unique characteristics of cyberspace {#cyberspace-characteristics}

JP 3-12 calls out several characteristics that set cyberspace apart from the physical domains and shape how operations must be planned:

- **Global, man-made, and dynamic.** Unlike the natural domains, cyberspace is entirely built by humans and is continually reconfigured — new networks, new protocols, new devices come online every day. What was true about a target yesterday may not be true today.
- **Speed of maneuver.** Effects propagate in milliseconds. Decision timelines in the physical domains (minutes, hours, days) do not translate.
- **Attribution challenges.** Adversaries can obscure their identity through proxies, compromised infrastructure, and persona manipulation. Attribution is often probabilistic, not definitive.
- **Near-universal connectivity.** A cyberspace effect on a seemingly isolated target can propagate through supply-chain and trust relationships. Blast-radius analysis is essential.
- **Low entry cost.** Nation-state capabilities and lone-actor capabilities overlap in a way they do not in the physical domains.

These characteristics drive why Chapter II's mission framework is structured the way it is, and why Chapter III's command relationships emphasize speed of decision.

## Chapter II — Cyberspace Operations and the Joint Functions {#chapter-ii-operations}

### The three cyberspace missions {#three-missions}

Every cyberspace operation the U.S. military conducts falls into one of three mission categories. Know these cold:

**1. DoD Information Network (DODIN) Operations.** Securing, configuring, operating, extending, maintaining, and sustaining the DODIN to create and preserve the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of the network itself. This is the everyday work of keeping DoD networks up and functional — patching, configuring, user management, hardware replacement, normal IT operations with a security mindset. DODIN Operations is the **baseline mission**; the other two build on it.

**2. Defensive Cyberspace Operations (DCO).** Missions to preserve the ability to use friendly cyberspace capabilities and to protect data, networks, cyberspace-enabled devices, and other designated systems by defeating specific threats that have either breached DODIN security measures or are actively attempting to do so. DCO is distinguished from DODIN Operations by being **threat-driven** rather than routine — you conduct DCO in response to a specific adversary action.

**3. Offensive Cyberspace Operations (OCO).** Missions intended to project power by the application of force in and through cyberspace. OCO creates effects in adversary-owned or adversary-controlled cyberspace. Requires specific legal authorization and is typically conducted or directed by USCYBERCOM.

### DCO-IDM vs DCO-RA {#dco-types}

This distinction is the second-most-tested fact in JP 3-12 and the one most candidates lose points on. Commit it to memory:

**DCO-IDM (Internal Defensive Measures).** Defensive actions taken **within** friendly, DoD-owned cyberspace. Examples: isolating an infected network segment, resetting compromised credentials, deploying additional sensors, killing malicious processes on a DoD-owned endpoint, reconfiguring a firewall to block a known-bad IP. The key word is **internal** — the entire effect stays inside the boundary you already own and control.

**DCO-RA (Response Actions).** Defensive actions taken **outside** friendly, DoD-owned cyberspace — i.e., in adversary-owned or neutral cyberspace — in response to an ongoing or imminent attack. Examples: scanning or disabling an adversary staging server being used to attack DoD networks, taking down a command-and-control node. Because DCO-RA operates outside the DoD perimeter, it requires **higher authorization** (typically up to the Secretary of Defense or the President depending on the target and effect) and is often indistinguishable in effect from OCO, even though the framing is defensive.

Memory aid: **IDM is Inside, RA is Reaching out.**

A common exam distractor swaps the definitions ("DCO-IDM is response actions in adversary space") — read each answer carefully.

### Cyberspace actions {#cyberspace-actions}

Within the mission areas, JP 3-12 identifies three specific **cyberspace actions** that describe *what* is being done technically:

- **Cyberspace ISR.** Intelligence collection conducted in and through cyberspace. May support any mission area.
- **Cyberspace Operational Preparation of the Environment (OPE).** Shaping activities to gain access, develop understanding, or pre-position capabilities. OPE is distinct from ISR because it may involve actively affecting the target (e.g., implanting a dormant capability) even though the primary effect has not yet occurred.
- **Cyberspace Attack.** Creating the intended denial, degradation, disruption, or destruction effect on the target. This is the kinetic verb of cyberspace.

A cyberspace action is the "what"; a mission (DODIN/DCO/OCO) is the "why." Any cyberspace action can in principle support any mission, though cyberspace attack rarely appears within DODIN Operations.

### Joint functions in cyberspace {#joint-functions}

JP 3-0 defines seven joint functions: Command and Control, Intelligence, Fires, Movement and Maneuver, Protection, Sustainment, and Information. JP 3-12 clarifies that **cyberspace operations contribute to every one of these joint functions** — not only Fires and Information. For example:

- Cyberspace operations enable **C2** through the DODIN.
- Cyberspace ISR directly feeds the **Intelligence** function.
- OCO and defensive cyber can produce **Fires** effects.
- Network resilience and DODIN security underpin **Protection**.

On the exam, a question asking "which joint function does cyberspace operations support" usually has "all of the above" or "all seven" as the correct answer.

## Chapter III — Authorities, Roles, and Responsibilities {#chapter-iii-authorities}

### USCYBERCOM {#uscybercom}

**United States Cyber Command (USCYBERCOM)** is a unified combatant command headquartered at Fort Meade, Maryland. As of 2018 USCYBERCOM was elevated from a sub-unified command under USSTRATCOM to a full unified command.

The language you need to know:

- USCYBERCOM is the **supported combatant command for cyberspace operations** when the operation's primary purpose is a cyberspace effect.
- USCYBERCOM is the **supporting combatant command** when another CCMD is the supported command and cyberspace effects are in support of that CCMD's objectives (e.g., USCENTCOM is supported for a regional operation, USCYBERCOM provides cyber support).
- USCYBERCOM operates the DODIN in partnership with service components and DoD agencies.

The **Commander, USCYBERCOM** is dual-hatted as the **Director of the National Security Agency (DIRNSA)**. This dual-hat arrangement is doctrine-driven, though an exam question may ask about the scope of each role independently.

### Service Cyber Components {#service-cyber-components}

Each military service provides forces to USCYBERCOM through a **Service Cyber Component (SCC)**:

- **Army Cyber Command (ARCYBER)** — the Army's SCC.
- **U.S. Fleet Cyber Command / U.S. Tenth Fleet (USFLTCYBERCOM / C10F)** — the Navy's SCC, headquartered at Fort Meade. Tenth Fleet is the numbered fleet under FLTCYBERCOM. **This is the one CWT Sailors work for most directly** — expect questions here.
- **Marine Corps Forces Cyberspace Command (MARFORCYBER)** — the Marine Corps SCC.
- **16th Air Force (Air Forces Cyber) / AFCYBER** — the Air Force's SCC, designated as the service information-warfare component. Formed in 2019 by merging 24th AF (cyber) with 25th AF (ISR); older references may still say "24th Air Force."
- **Space Force** — Space Force is building out a cyber contribution; doctrine continues to evolve.

Each SCC provides **Cyber Mission Force (CMF)** teams to USCYBERCOM. CMF team categories include:

- **Cyber Protection Teams (CPTs)** — execute DCO to defend priority DoD missions. See CPT ORG 3-33.4 on this Bib for more detail.
- **Combat Mission Teams (CMTs)** and **Combat Support Teams (CSTs)** — provide offensive cyber support to regional combatant commands.
- **National Mission Teams (NMTs)** and **National Support Teams (NSTs)** — defend the nation against strategic cyber threats, directed by USCYBERCOM.

### The DODIN {#dodin}

The **DoD Information Network (DODIN)** is the globally interconnected, end-to-end set of information capabilities and associated processes for collecting, processing, storing, disseminating, and managing information on demand to warfighters, policymakers, and support personnel. The DODIN includes the SIPRNET, NIPRNET, JWICS, and many other interconnected enclaves. Operating the DODIN is a shared responsibility among USCYBERCOM, DISA, the service components, and agency-level CIOs.

The **DoD CIO** sets policy and standards for the DODIN. **DISA** provides much of the underlying infrastructure and services. **USCYBERCOM** directs DODIN Operations through JFHQ-DODIN (Joint Force Headquarters – DODIN).

## Chapter IV — Planning, Execution, and Assessment {#chapter-iv-planning}

### Integration with the Joint Planning Process {#jpp-integration}

Cyberspace operations are **not planned in isolation**. They are integrated into the standard Joint Planning Process (JPP) described in JP 5-0, with cyberspace-specific considerations layered in at each step:

1. **Planning Initiation** — cyberspace planners join the planning team early.
2. **Mission Analysis** — identify cyberspace-relevant tasks (specified, implied, essential), critical factors, and commander's intent for cyberspace effects.
3. **COA Development** — develop courses of action that include cyberspace effects.
4. **COA Analysis and Wargaming** — wargame adversary cyberspace actions and friendly responses.
5. **COA Comparison** — weigh cyberspace-enabled COAs against alternatives.
6. **COA Approval** — decision; cyberspace authorities may need to be delegated or escalated based on chosen COA.
7. **Plan or Order Development** — produce the plan or order with a cyberspace annex (Appendix 3 to Annex C, typically).

### Cyberspace effects planning {#effects-planning}

JP 3-12 adopts the **effects-based approach** consistent with JP 3-0 and JP 5-0:

- Define the **desired end state** in terms of what adversary capabilities or behaviors should be denied, degraded, disrupted, or destroyed — the **4 Ds**.
- Identify the **cyberspace target set** and map it to the three layers.
- Deconflict with other effects (fires, IO, electronic warfare).
- Sequence actions with intended physical effects.

Targeting in cyberspace is governed by the standard joint targeting cycle (JP 3-60), with additional considerations for intelligence gain/loss, attribution risk, blast radius, and legal review.

### Authorities and deconfliction {#authorities-deconfliction}

Because cyberspace effects can propagate, **deconfliction** is essential. Three common deconfliction pressures:

1. **With other U.S. cyber actors** — CIA, FBI, DHS, other DoD components may already be on the target.
2. **With intelligence equities** — an OCO action against a collection platform can destroy a SIGINT or HUMINT stream.
3. **With partner-nation operations** — coalition cyberspace operations require standing agreements.

The **Technical Deconfliction** authority and process are described in USCC INST 3000.14 on this Bib.

### Assessment {#assessment}

Assessment is continuous, not just end-of-mission. JP 3-12 requires:

- **Measures of Performance (MOP)** — did we execute the planned action?
- **Measures of Effectiveness (MOE)** — did the action produce the intended effect on the target?
- **Battle Damage Assessment (BDA) adapted for cyberspace** — what exactly was denied, degraded, disrupted, or destroyed, and for how long?

Cyberspace BDA is harder than physical BDA because effects can be transient, partial, or silently reversed by the adversary.

## Common exam gotchas {#gotchas}

Each of these trips up candidates routinely:

1. **DCO-IDM vs DCO-RA** — inside vs outside friendly cyberspace. Covered above.
2. **"Cyberspace" vs "cybersecurity"** — cybersecurity is a *subset* of DODIN Operations focused on confidentiality/integrity/availability. Cyberspace operations is the broader doctrinal concept including DCO and OCO.
3. **Supported vs supporting** — USCYBERCOM switches roles based on mission primary purpose. Read the question carefully.
4. **Three layers named correctly** — physical, logical, cyber-persona. Distractors often include "operational," "access," "spectrum," "application," or "transport" — none of those are JP 3-12 layers.
5. **Number of mission areas** — three (DODIN, DCO, OCO). Four or five almost always wrong.
6. **Tenth Fleet affiliation** — Tenth Fleet is under Fleet Cyber Command, not Naval Information Forces (NAVIFOR). NAVIFOR is the TYCOM that provides administrative-control manning to the IW community; operational control of cyber forces runs through FLTCYBERCOM to USCYBERCOM.
7. **JP 3-12 is joint doctrine, not Navy doctrine** — NWP 3-12 is the Navy publication. Make sure the question is asking about the right document.
